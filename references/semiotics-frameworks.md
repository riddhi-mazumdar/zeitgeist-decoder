# Semiotics frameworks for cultural decoding

This is the toolkit for Phase 2 of the workflow. Three frameworks, in order of use.

## 1. Roland Barthes' three layers of meaning

Source: *Mythologies* (1957), *Elements of Semiology* (1964).

Every cultural signal carries meaning at three layers. The job of the strategist is to name all three, because brands and creators usually operate at the wrong layer — most marketing speaks to denotation when the cultural action is happening at the myth layer.

### Denotation
The literal, observable thing. What a camera would record. No interpretation.

Example signal: a 24-year-old woman in Mumbai wearing a "Run DMC" t-shirt.
- Denotation: a cotton t-shirt with the words "Run DMC" printed in red and black, worn with high-waisted jeans.

### Connotation
The associated cultural meanings the sign carries. The first layer of interpretation. Most "trend reports" stop here and call it insight. It is not insight, it is observation.

- Connotation: 80s hip-hop, Adidas Superstars, a specific kind of music-nerd cool, vintage rap merchandise, possibly thrifted, possibly bought new at H&M.

### Myth
The ideology the sign naturalizes — the worldview it makes feel obvious and natural. This is where the strategic opportunity lives, because brands that can speak to the myth layer build cultural relevance. Brands stuck at connotation only buy reference, not relevance.

- Myth: that authenticity is rented from the past, that one can claim cultural credibility through visual citation of a movement one was not part of, that nostalgia for a pre-internet music economy is itself a form of taste.

**When using Barthes in a decode, always name all three layers explicitly.** The denotation is to discipline you against jumping to interpretation. The connotation is the observable cultural meaning. The myth is the strategic gold.

## 2. Raymond Williams' residual / dominant / emergent

Source: *Marxism and Literature* (1977), particularly the chapter "Dominant, Residual, and Emergent."

Williams argued that at any given moment, three kinds of cultural elements coexist. The framework lets you classify a signal in time, which dictates what kind of brand action is possible.

### Residual
Formed in the past but still active in the present. Often functions in opposition to the dominant culture. Acting on residual signals means working with nostalgia, heritage, or revival.

Examples (as of 2026):
- Vinyl records
- Hand-written letters
- The "village shop" aesthetic in urban cafés
- Y2K fashion (which began emergent around 2020, then went dominant, and is now residual)

Brand strategy on residual: heritage plays, nostalgia campaigns, craft positioning. Risks looking out-of-touch if the residual signal is in genuine decline rather than stable.

### Dominant
The current mainstream. What feels normal, obvious, default. Most brands operate here because it is the lowest-risk territory and the highest-volume one.

Examples (as of 2026):
- Short-form vertical video as the default content unit
- AI-assisted productivity as a workplace norm
- The "quiet luxury" aesthetic (post-Brunello Cucinelli, post-Succession)
- The aesthetic of authenticity (lowercase typography, "unfiltered" content)

Brand strategy on dominant: high-reach, broad appeal, but no differentiation. Sharp (see strategy-frameworks.md) would say you want to be salient inside the dominant — distinctive within the mainstream, not against it.

### Emergent
Genuinely new meanings, values, practices that are not yet codified. Distinguishable from a passing fad by whether the underlying tension or need it addresses is durable. Emergent signals are high-risk, high-reward.

Crucial test: is this emergent or is it merely novel? A truly emergent signal addresses a contradiction in the dominant culture that the dominant culture cannot resolve. A merely novel signal is just a new aesthetic with no underlying tension.

Examples that turned out to be genuinely emergent (in hindsight):
- "Quiet quitting" (2022) — emergent because it named a real worker/employer contradiction.
- The creator economy (2018-ish) — emergent because it resolved a contradiction between media gatekeeping and direct audience access.

Examples that turned out to be merely novel:
- NFT profile pictures (2021–2022) — novel aesthetic, no durable contradiction resolved.
- Most "cores" (cottagecore, gorpcore, etc.) — visual aesthetics without underlying cultural tension.

Brand strategy on emergent: pioneering position if you get it right, embarrassing if you get it wrong. Recommend acting on emergent signals only when the brand has a structural reason to be there (a product, a leader, a heritage that lines up).

### How to classify

Ask: "Does this signal feel like a hangover, the air, or a draft from a window we haven't found yet?" Hangover = residual. Air = dominant. Draft = emergent.

Cross-check: if the signal is being widely discussed in mainstream media, it is at least dominant by now. Trend writers are not early. By the time *Vogue* covers it, it is dominant.

## 3. Jean-Marie Floch's semiotic square (advanced — use selectively)

Source: *Semiotics, Marketing and Communication* (1990).

Use the square when a cultural signal has a clear opposite, and you want to find unowned territory in the category. Adapted from Greimas' formal semiotic square, Floch applied it to brand positioning.

Given a term S1, you can map:
- **S1**: the term itself
- **S2**: its opposite (contrary)
- **not-S1**: its contradiction (negation)
- **not-S2**: the contradiction of the opposite

The four corners create four positions. The interesting strategic question is: which corners are owned by which brands, and which corner is empty?

### Worked example: skincare

- S1 = "results-driven, clinical" (e.g., The Ordinary, La Roche-Posay)
- S2 = "sensorial, ritual" (e.g., Aesop, Diptyque)
- not-S1 = "anti-results, anti-clinical" (e.g., Lush — playful, performative, anti-science aesthetic)
- not-S2 = "anti-ritual, fast" (e.g., basic drugstore brands, Cetaphil — utilitarian, no ceremony)

A brand entering this category should pick a corner, not float in the middle. The square tells you which corners are crowded and which are empty.

### When to use the square

Use it when:
- The category has a clear values axis (clinical/sensorial, mass/premium, traditional/modern, individualist/collectivist)
- The user is trying to position a brand or campaign against a competitor set
- The decode in Phase 2 has surfaced a tension

Skip it when:
- The signal is not yet positionable (still too emergent)
- The category does not have a clean opposition
- The user just wants a fast read on cultural meaning

## Applying the three frameworks together

In Phase 2 of the workflow, the output should look like:

```
SIGNAL: [one-line description from Phase 1]

DENOTATION: [what it literally is]
CONNOTATION: [what it culturally references]
MYTH: [what worldview it naturalizes]

TIME CLASSIFICATION: [residual / dominant / emergent]
JUSTIFICATION: [one sentence — why this and not another]

(IF APPLICABLE) SEMIOTIC SQUARE:
  S1: [...]
  S2 (opposite): [...]
  not-S1 (negation): [...]
  not-S2 (negation of opposite): [...]
  Unowned corner: [...]
```

This block is the foundation for Phase 3 (permission) and Phase 4 (search). Do not move on without it.
