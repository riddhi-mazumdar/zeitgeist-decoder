# Strategy frameworks for brand permission and positioning

This is the toolkit for Phase 3 of the workflow. Four practitioners, used in combination. Each one is a corrective for a typical failure mode in cultural strategy.

## 1. Byron Sharp — How Brands Grow (mental availability)

Source: *How Brands Grow* (2010), *How Brands Grow Part 2* (2015), Ehrenberg-Bass Institute.

### The single most uncomfortable idea

Most growth comes from light buyers, not loyalists. Brands grow by reaching more category buyers, not by deepening relationships with existing ones. This means cultural relevance is only useful if it builds **mental availability** — the probability that the brand comes to mind in a buying situation.

### What this means for cultural strategy

When evaluating whether a brand should act on a cultural signal, ask:
1. Will acting on this signal increase the number of people who think of the brand in a buying situation? Or will it only deepen affinity among those who already buy?
2. Does the cultural action create or reinforce a **distinctive brand asset** (a colour, sound, character, shape, phrase, format that uniquely belongs to the brand)? Or is it a generic execution any brand could run?
3. Does the cultural action align with the **category entry points** the brand wants to own (the moments, needs, occasions when category purchase is triggered)?

### Permission implications

Sharp would say: a brand has permission to talk about almost any cultural signal, as long as the execution builds distinctive brand assets and links to category entry points. The "should we?" question is less about cultural fit and more about whether the execution will be remembered and attributed to the right brand.

The failure mode Sharp protects against: beautifully crafted, culturally on-point work that no one can remember was made by your brand. The Heineken effect. The award-winning ad whose brand recall is 4%.

## 2. Rory Sutherland — alchemy and irrational positioning

Source: *Alchemy* (2019), Ogilvy UK Vice Chairman, Behavioural Science Practice.

### The single most useful idea

The opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea. Logic gets you to the same answer as everyone else. Useful asymmetry comes from being deliberately illogical — choosing the more expensive option, the slower service, the smaller selection, the more inconvenient location.

### What this means for cultural strategy

When evaluating a cultural signal, ask the inverse-question test:
- What is the obvious brand response to this signal?
- What is the opposite response?
- Is the opposite response also defensible? If yes, is it a better fit for your brand than the obvious one?

Examples of the inverse working:
- When every D2C brand went "minimal sans-serif on cream background," Liquid Death went the opposite (heavy metal aesthetic, water as transgression). Same category, opposite execution, distinctive brand asset created.
- When every B2B SaaS brand went "we are the platform for...", Linear went the opposite (deliberately spartan, no jargon, treats users as adults).

### Permission implications

Sutherland would say: permission is overrated. What matters is whether the brand can credibly own the opposite of whatever the category default is. A brand has permission if its action surprises and the surprise is defensible.

The failure mode Sutherland protects against: doing the obvious thing because the data supports it, and being undifferentiated as a result.

## 3. David Ogilvy — research, headlines, the long copy bet

Source: *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963), *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983).

### The single most relevant idea

The consumer is not a moron, she is your wife. Speak to her with respect. Lead with a strong, specific promise that has news in it. Long copy outsells short copy when the audience is already considering a purchase.

### What this means for cultural strategy

When the cultural action involves written communication (brief, campaign, content), apply Ogilvy's checks:
- Is the headline specific? (Not "Refresh your skincare routine" — specific, like "The serum that fades dark spots in 28 days, used by 80% of dermatologists.")
- Is there news in the proposition? Cultural relevance without news is decoration.
- Does the copy assume an intelligent reader? (No talking down, no "fun, flirty, fearless" rule-of-three.)
- Is the brand named in the first sentence/frame? (Sharp would also insist on this — distinctive assets early.)

### Permission implications

Ogilvy would say: a brand earns permission through specificity and respect. Vague cultural commentary fails. Specific, well-researched cultural insight that respects the reader's intelligence builds permission.

The failure mode Ogilvy protects against: thin, decorative, "voice"-led copy with no substance.

## 4. Douglas Holt — cultural branding (iconic brands resolve contradictions)

Source: *How Brands Become Icons* (2004).

### The single most powerful idea

Iconic brands become icons not by being beloved or having strong personalities, but by resolving a sharp cultural contradiction through their identity myth. The brand becomes a story the culture tells itself to manage a tension it cannot otherwise resolve.

Examples Holt uses:
- Marlboro: resolved the 1950s tension between corporate-male conformity and the lost American frontier. The cowboy was the myth that let men reconcile being office workers with the masculine ideal.
- Nike: resolved the tension between hyper-individualist achievement ideology and structural barriers to actually achieving anything. "Just do it" said: the system is rigged, but personal will transcends.
- Coke (mid-century): resolved the tension between modern American capitalism and small-town communitarian values.

### What this means for cultural strategy

When evaluating whether a brand has permission for a cultural signal, ask:
1. What is the cultural contradiction underneath this signal? (Most signals are surface manifestations of deeper contradictions.)
2. Does this brand have a credible identity that can resolve, or speak to, that contradiction?
3. If yes, the brand has not just permission, it has **mandate**.

Example application: the rise of "soft life" / anti-hustle content among urban Indian millennials is a surface signal of a deeper contradiction — between the parental-generation expectation of upward striving and a generation that has watched striving produce burnout without proportional reward. A brand that can credibly resolve this contradiction (a slow-fashion label, a savings product positioned around freedom-not-acquisition, a wellness brand grounded in actual rest rather than performance rest) has mandate. A brand that just borrows the aesthetic does not.

### Permission implications

Holt would say: cultural permission is binary at the highest level. Either the brand can resolve the underlying contradiction or it cannot. If it cannot, the most it can do is participate visually (which is what most brands do — they buy the aesthetic, not the meaning).

The failure mode Holt protects against: aesthetic-without-meaning cultural participation. Brands that show up to the party without understanding why the party is happening.

## How to combine them in Phase 3

The permission map produced in Phase 3 should be informed by all four lenses:

| Question | Framework | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Will this execution be remembered and attributed correctly? | Sharp | Whether the cultural action builds the brand or just decorates it |
| What is the non-obvious / inverse take on this signal? | Sutherland | Where the differentiation opportunity is |
| Is the proposition specific and newsworthy? | Ogilvy | Whether the writing earns the reader's time |
| Does the brand have mandate to resolve the underlying contradiction? | Holt | Whether the action is borrowed aesthetic or genuine cultural claim |

A green-light permission verdict requires at least three of the four lenses to be positive. Two or fewer = yellow at best.

A red-light verdict typically comes from Holt — the brand has no credible claim to the underlying contradiction — or from Sharp — the execution will not be attributed to the brand even if it lands.

## Output format for Phase 3

```
PERMISSION AUDIT for [brand] on [signal]

NATIVE OWNERS: [who has full permission]
ADJACENT PERMISSION: [who can play credibly]
NO-FLY ZONE: [categories where this would be extractive]

[BRAND]'S POSITION: [native / adjacent / no-fly]
JUSTIFICATION: [one paragraph drawing on at least 2 frameworks]

VERDICT: [GREEN / YELLOW / RED]
IF GREEN: the specific angle the brand should take
IF YELLOW: the conditions under which it could work
IF RED: the alternative recommendation
```

Hand this to Phase 4. The keyword research is informed by the audience and angle decided here.
